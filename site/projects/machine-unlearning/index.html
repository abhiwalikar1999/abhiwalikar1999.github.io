Machine Unlearning for Face Recognition

How do you check that a face recognition model has really forgotten someone? In a team of three, we developed a new evaluation score for machine unlearning and tested it across three unlearning methods, seven face recognition models and 5.8 million images.

01Problem

Under Article 17 of the GDPR, people can ask for their personal data to be erased. Deleting the records doesn't remove their influence on a trained model, and retraining after every request is expensive.

Machine unlearning edits the trained model instead, which raises the question of how to verify that it worked. In face recognition this is hard: models map faces to embeddings, and aggregate accuracy can hide what actually changed for the forgotten identities.

An existing measure, the Forget Score (FS), compares the model's embeddings before and after unlearning. We developed a new score, the Adversarial Forget Score (AFS), and measured whether it separates forgotten from retained identities more clearly.

02Experimental setup

DatasetMS1M-V2, 5,822,653 face images. Scores are computed on a fixed sample of 10,000 retained images.
Models (7)ArcFace 18 / 50 / 100, AdaFace 18 / 50 / 100, DQMFace 100
RADOne identity forgotten at a time, averaged over five identities, with sampling sizes from 10 images up to the full retain set.
UCE and SVDApplied to linearised models, with 1 to 1,000 identities forgotten jointly.
BaselineForget Score (FS), computed on the same identities and samples.
ComputeNVIDIA H100

03Results

AFS exceeded FS in 40 of the 41 completed RAD configurations. The one exception was DQMFace100 with only 10 sampled images (AFS 0.805 vs FS 0.831). At a sampling size of 10,000, AFS also rose with backbone depth for both ArcFace and AdaFace.

Accuracy alone isn't enough

Under RAD, forget-set accuracy stayed between 99.37% and 100% for ArcFace and AdaFace, while both scores showed substantial changes in the representations. The scores and accuracy measure different effects, so they have to be read together.

Where the two scores disagree

When identities are forgotten jointly with UCE and SVD, both scores saturate at 1.000 for small requests. For large requests they can diverge sharply, especially for UCE on ArcFace:

ConfigurationForget acc.FSAFS
ArcFace50, UCE, 500 identities49.79%0.8040.171
ArcFace50, UCE, 1,000 identities73.95%0.6160.043
ArcFace100, UCE, 1,000 identities94.05%0.6790.303
AdaFace50, SVD, 1,000 identities80.43%0.2610.481

These cases flag where to look rather than explain why. Per-identity analysis would be needed to find which identities cause the disagreement.

04Runtime tuning

The score has a search setting, K, and the runtime of a full experiment grows with it. We started from the value used in prior work (K = 10), and I measured what each setting actually buys before we picked one.

  • K = 10 → 5 cut a full run from 1,215 s to 690 s (−43%), while AFS changed by less than 0.001 (0.8681 → 0.8674).
  • K = 1 was fast too and scored slightly higher, but it explores too little to be a reliable setting.
  • K = 5 balanced cost and coverage, and it held up across all of our experiments.

05Lessons learned

  • Accuracy can hide representation change

    Forget accuracy near 100% next to large embedding shifts showed why evaluation has to look inside the model, not only at its outputs.

  • Cost is a design parameter

    The same score at roughly half the runtime came from measuring the trade-off rather than copying the default from prior work.

  • A score is a lens, not a proof

    Where FS and AFS disagree, the disagreement itself is the finding. It points to configurations that need closer, per-identity analysis.
